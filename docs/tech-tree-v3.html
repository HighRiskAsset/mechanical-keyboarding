Tech Tree v3

Six ores, nine kinds of lesson machine, seven tiers. Every lesson is a recipe: what goes into a machine sets its alphabet, the machine sets its grammar. Mines start at two keys and grow by upgrade; automation follows mastery and then runs on its own; belts carry what machines make; and by the end everything you have mastered works without you, while everything new still needs your hands.

The rules that generate the tree

Everything below is derived from these. If a recipe or a tier disagrees with a rule, the recipe is wrong.

1 · An ore is a finger. A Mk is a reach. locked

Iron, copper, stone are the index finger's home, top and bottom rows (the six most frequent letters). Quartz is the middle finger, coal the ring, oil the pinky. An ore's Mk adds the next key-pair on that finger. Pairs are mirror keys — same finger, both hands — sorted by frequency. Mk is per ore, not per mine: every iron mine drills the same keys.

Iron Mk1 = а о (F J) · Mk2 = п р (G H)

2 · Alphabet = union of the inputs.

A material's letter set is the union of what went into it, computed live from the recipe graph and the current Mk levels. Feed a Smelter iron + copper and it drills syllables over а о е н; iron + stone, over а о и т. Upgrade iron and every downstream lesson widens.

bronze @T0 = {а о е н} → @T1 = {а о п р е н}

3 · Grammar = machine kind. Ratio = variance only.

Mine → letters. Smelter → syllables. Foundry → clusters. Constructor → words. Molder → endings. Assembler → phrases. Fastener → punctuated sentences. Crane → capitals. Manufacturer → pages. Each grammar has a minimum alphabet. Recipes have quantities; the quantity may tilt sampling (3 iron + 1 coal → iron letters come up more often) but never filters a pool — the word bank stays the full union, the tilt is capped at 3:1, and it switches off entirely when a pool is small.

syllables ≥4 (≥1 vowel + 1 consonant) · words ≥8 & ≥25 real words · sentences ≥20

4 · Every deeper recipe carries a flux.

From the Molder on, alphabets are the full unlocked set — but every recipe still takes one raw ore or alloy alongside its parts. That flux sets the lesson's focus (its letters, its ending family, its word pool) and ties the deep machines to the old nodes.

2 parts + 1 stone → moldings drilling -ть -ить -ом -им

5 · You choose the recipe at the machine. Kinds are templates.

(Amended 19 Aug 2026.) A machine's hold-Space menu lists every recipe it offers; arrows pick, a second hold confirms; the choice sticks to that machine and shows bright above it. No silent switching: an unpayable choice runs dry and shows ✗. Several instances of a kind exist. On its own (phase 3), a machine makes its chosen recipe from what its belts bring.

the chosen row is bright · ✗ = can't pay

6 · Everything is built from your bag, at the place.

No Hub, no kits, no contracts. Hold Space at a plot, a node or a machine and its menu opens: arrows choose, hold Space confirms. A plot lists the machines you could build there (greyed when you can't afford them; a machine appears once you've held the materials it costs, so high tiers stay out of sight until you reach them). A mine lists Mk, ⚙, collect, feed, spool. A closed pass or bridge lists its repair. Nothing is locked behind a tier number: order comes from the letter ladder alone, pacing from prices that ask for later materials plus the readiness bar. Each row shows its curriculum gate (readiness of the letters it waits on, at the bar of the tier the pair belongs to) ∧ price. Materials pay for placement, never for progress.

Mk2 · п р · 80 iron + 30 bronze · ▮▮▮▯ в л

7 · Automation runs on the clock. Skill never does.

What you have mastered works without you: automated machines and belts run in real time — while you walk, while you build, while the tab is hidden — so old lessons are truly obsolete. "Idle" production (what accrues while you aren't typing) is bounded: buffers cap at ≈100 and then the machine simply waits, and the current tier's goods are always hand-made — a kind gets ⚙ one tier after it arrives — so waiting can fill the inputs of your next lesson but can never pay for your next purchase. Letters, readiness and tier bars only ever move by typing.

smelter job ≈ 3 s · belt 2 tiles/s · buffers 100

Badge = tier the key is introduced. Number-row keys are punctuation (Fastener Mk2/Mk3). Shift first appears at T3 for one key — comma, the layout's signature hurdle — and generalizes to capitals at T5.

The factory simulation

Machines have insides. Belts carry things. Automation runs on the clock; your hands run one station at a time. This is the model everything else sits on — stated once, precisely.

Machines

  • Buffers. Every machine has an input buffer per material it accepts and one output buffer. Generous caps (≈100): a full machine pauses, nothing spills, nothing spoils.
  • Hand work. Dock and type. Each correct keystroke consumes inputs at the recipe ratio — from the machine's own buffers first, then from your bag — and advances that station only. Output goes to your bag, unless a belt leaves the machine, in which case it rolls onto the belt.
  • Automated work. With ⚙, sticky letters and a full input set in its buffers, the machine runs a timed job and emits to the exit belt or its output buffer. It never reaches into your bag, and it never needs you.
  • You can still carry. Hold Space at any machine to collect its output buffer, or to feed its input buffers from your bag. Early game is carrying; late game is belts; both always work.
  • Instances. Kinds are templates. A second Smelter is a plot purchase; its recipe is whatever arrives. How many the player builds is up to them; the map offers as many nodes and plots as the tree can use (nodes per ore placed farther out each tier, plots opened region by region) — never a scarce resource. A typical full build lands near 13 mines, 12 processors, 40 belts — the number the hour estimates assume.

Rates and bounds (placeholders)

  • Real time, always. Automated machines and belts run whether you type, walk, build or stand still — and while the tab is hidden: on return the game fast-forwards the elapsed time (bounded by buffers), so leaving is never punished and never rewarded beyond full buffers. Working a station by hand advances that station only.
  • Belts: 2 tiles/s, one item per tile — a belt's ceiling is 2 items/s. Pipes the same.
  • Jobs: mine 2 s/ore · Smelter 3 · Foundry 4 · Constructor 4 · Molder 5 · Assembler 6 · Fastener 6 · Crane 7 · Manufacturer 10. Quick, not immediate.
  • Automation is parallel, not fast. A fluent typist out-produces any single automated bench; the factory wins because every automated machine runs at once, forever.
  • Idle is bounded. Buffers cap at ~100; heavy modules come only from the hand-worked Manufacturer; the current tier's goods are always hand-made. Leave the game overnight and you return to full inputs for your next lesson — never to a purchase you didn't type for.
  • Tuning target: one mine feeds about one consumer's appetite for its ore. The player never computes rates; they see a hungry machine and build another mine.
  • Optional feel layer, undecided: the factory runs ×1.5 while you are typing anywhere — the belts pick up your rhythm. Not a separate power station: that would be one more lesson-less machine competing with the lessons.

Two gates — the curriculum gate (every unlocked letter at readiness; the row shows a gauge for the letters it waits on) and the previous tier's bar — then the price. Prices are placeholders showing the pattern: the ore's own material (typed by hand, right before its new keys arrive) plus a good from the current tier. Extra mines of an ore are bought at unbuilt nodes and inherit the ore's Mk.

Automation: one rule, no special cases

The current game automates tier-1 benches with a material-paid ⚙ gated by sticky letter mastery. v3 keeps that and extends it to every machine with a single rule.

A machine runs a recipe by itself, in real time, iff: it has its ⚙ · every letter in that recipe's alphabet is sticky-mastered · its input buffers hold a full set at the recipe ratio. Otherwise that recipe is hand-work. Automated recipes refuse labor; un-automated recipes at the same machine still accept it.

What falls out of the rule

  • Retool is not a mechanic. Buy a Mk → the ore's alphabet gains two un-sticky letters → every mine of that ore is hand-work again. Master them → they resume. No fee, no toggle.
  • New recipes at an automated machine are still lessons. A Smelter automated for bronze at T1 still hand-drills black iron syllables at T3, because я й з aren't sticky yet.
  • Retool doesn't cascade. Downstream keeps its ⚙; it simply starves while the retooled mines' belts idle. Bounded backtracking: one ore, one pair.
  • The whole factory ends automated — except the Manufacturer, which is you.

When ⚙ becomes purchasable

  • Mines: as soon as the ore's current keys are sticky; per mine instance. Cost: its ore + the tier's good. Nothing forces it — you automate because it ends the walk.
  • Processors: one tier of hand work first — ⚙ appears once the bar that closes the kind's arrival tier is passed. Smelter → T1, Foundry/Constructor → T2, Molder/Assembler → T3, Fastener → T4, Crane → T6, Manufacturer → after the finish (free-play only).
  • Rate: seconds per job as in the simulation table; passing tier bars 2 and 4 speeds the factory 20% each time.

Transport: belts and pipes

A belt is a conveyor from one machine's outlet to another machine's inlet. Anything the source makes — by your hands or on the grid — rolls onto it. Belts decide whether you walk; buffers decide whether machines wait; neither asks you to plan a layout.

The rules

  • Unlocked with tier 1 (the T0 bar passed) — "grid power". Any machine can be a source from then on; automation is not required. Pipes arrive with the oil derrick (T3): same rules, different skin, and the only piped resource.
  • Built by spool & socket, above, only into a machine that accepts that material. Inlets = the kind's arity: one belt per input slot (Constructor 1, Smelter/Foundry/Molder/Assembler/Fastener/Crane 2, Manufacturer 3). Outlets: mines 1, processors 2 — a processor can split its output once (round-robin); a mine feeds one consumer, which is what grows the pyramid. No cap on the number of belts. A belt carries only what its consumer accepts — the outlet filters — so belts never clog.
  • Routing and congestion. One belt per tile; the router takes the shortest free path. Choke points are authored into the map — bridges, gaps between rocks — so choosing which lines get the bridge is the same bounded strategy as choosing plots. Belts can be removed for half refund; an overpass arrives at tier 4 to cross an existing line.
  • Cost: free for now. Automation and the machines themselves are the material sinks; if the economy later needs another, a distance price in the tier's trade good is the obvious one to add.
  • Flow: 2 tiles/s, one item per tile. A belt from a mine that yields every 2 s is a quarter full — plenty; a mine feeding three smelters is not, and that is the cue to build another mine.
  • Inspect: docking shows the machine's belts as icon rows (source → this, this → target). Belts are removed and half-refunded when a machine is relocated.

The build-out (~40 belts, roughly in order)

  • T1 — iron, copper, stone, quartz → Smelter A · iron #2 → Smelter B (quartz iron) · quartz → Foundry · Smelters → Foundry · Smelters/Foundry → Constructor.
  • T2 — coal → Smelter B · iron #3 + coal → Smelter C (steel) · Foundry → Constructor #2 · Constructors → Molder · Constructor → Assembler · Smelter → Assembler · iron, stone → Molder (flux).
  • T3 — oil ⇒ Smelter, Foundry, Fastener, Molder (pipes) · Assembler → Fastener · oil #2 ⇒ Smelter B.
  • T4 — coal #2 → Fastener, Molder · copper #2 → Molder, Smelter C.
  • T5 — Fastener → Crane · oil ⇒ Crane.
  • T6 — Crane, Molder, Constructor → Manufacturer. The last belts are the long hauls across the whole map — the megaproject.

Evaluated against the tree

  • Logistics can't lead the curriculum. A belt only carries what a machine makes; the deep machines only make things once their alphabets exist and their letters are sticky. The network follows mastery by about half a tier.
  • Retools pull you back exactly one ore. Its mines leave the grid, their belts idle, downstream starves — you hand-type the new pair, it comes back. Nothing downstream is lost or rebuilt.
  • The inverted pyramid is real. Many mines, fewer smelters, one Molder: the belt count and the map both grow at the base, exactly where the letters live.
  • Buffers keep it a typing game. Nothing spoils, nothing spills, nothing needs babysitting; every management act is a hold-Space at the place — collect, feed, spool. Congestion is a lever (which lines get the bridge), never a wall (red ghost → carry; overpass later).
  • ~40 spool-walks over ~32 h — one every 45 minutes, each ~30 s plus a cost you were already producing. Enough to feel like building; not enough to compete with typing.

Rejected alternatives, and why

  1. Keystroke-tick clock (the factory advances only while you type, anywhere). Keeps the letter of invariant 1 but has a structural flaw: when the frontier machine is waiting on inputs and everything upstream is automated and refuses labor, there is nowhere to type — the factory deadlocks until you go work an old lesson, which is exactly what automation was supposed to end. It also turns every walk and every belt-laying into dead time. Real-time automation with bounded idle fixes both.
  2. A power station (a machine you type at to speed the whole factory). One more lesson-less station competing with lessons; if the feel is wanted, a global boost while typing anywhere gives it without a building.
  3. Keystroke-laid belts (type to extend the belt). Charming, but not a lesson — the operator should be typing lessons.
  4. Manual routing. Invariant: constrained building; the player runs machines, doesn't plan layouts.
  5. Throughput math (Mk belts, splitters, ratios to balance). Tune so one mine ≈ one consumer instead; the player reads hunger, not numbers.
  6. Hub contracts for anything. Every price lives where the thing happens. A guidance checklist (what to build next) is later UI, not a mechanic; contracts may return later for variety.

Two clocks: the keyboard is learned before the speed arrives

Players will know every key before they hold 30–40 WPM. The tree is built so that stops mattering: the letter clock runs out around T5; a second clock takes over.

The skill clock (T0–T5)

  • Pairs unlock on readiness — 30+ clean samples at 25 WPM-equivalent latency, ≥97% — so keys cannot arrive faster than a modest fluency on the previous ones. Twenty introductions × ~50 min ≈ 17 h.
  • Tier bars hold the gates at 12 → 28 WPM-equivalent on every unlocked letter. They cannot be rushed; they are the pace.
  • Every Mk retool and every flux slot is review of an older bench with new material — the "some backtracking, not far, not often" budget, one ore at a time.

The volume clock (T5 to the finish, and beyond)

  • The factory feeds you; you finish it. Once everything is automated and belted, the whole chain runs on its own up to the Manufacturer — and heavy modules exist only because you type pages there. Finishing the game ("launch") needs K heavy modules (K ≈ 200 as a placeholder, tuned so it takes ~6–8 h at ~30 WPM) plus every letter past bar 35.
  • What you type there is a content slot, not a mechanic. Pages come from a per-course pages file: real prose, trivia, easter eggs, the machines talking to you, a plot that turns — written separately from engineering. The engine only cares that pages are graded by length and punctuation density.
  • After the finish: free-play with bars raised (50+ WPM), speed runs, hint-free night shifts, and any further pages you write. The factory keeps humming.

The material ladder is the grammar ladder

One figure, one claim: each production form maps to one level of motor chunking, and a raw ore or alloy rides along into every deeper recipe as the flux that sets focus.

Does "inputs set the recipe" actually train well?

Your idea, evaluated against progression and learning. Verdict: yes — with six guard rails, all of which are already in the rules above.

Where it works

  • Variety without more kinds. 9 kinds × ~30 curated recipes ≈ 47 lessons, and every Mk upgrade rewrites every downstream lesson. Bronze at T0 has 4 letters; at T1 it has 6. The same smelter is worth walking back to because it changed.
  • The walk is the focus dial. Carry coal into the Molder and it teaches coal endings; carry stone and it teaches -ть. Agency without a menu — invariant 2 holds.
  • Curriculum honesty is automatic. A material can only union letters the player has unlocked, because Mk levels ARE the unlocks. No recipe can leak a key early.
  • Backtracking is a property of the graph. Flux inputs tie deep machines to old nodes; retools pull you to exactly one ore; the ≤2-tier reach falls out of which ores get upgraded when.
  • The lesson set is well-rounded. positions → letters → syllables → clusters → words → endings → phrases → punctuated sentences → capitals → pages: ten grammars, each with a minimum alphabet so nothing arrives before it can be meaningful.

Where it strains — and the guard rail

  1. Ambiguity. A full bag makes three recipes affordable at once. → Rule 5: weakest letters first, then newest; the active row is marked; carrying only what you want overrides. On its own, a machine makes what its belts bring.
  2. Vowel poverty. Iron holds а о; quartz has no vowel until T3; late ores are consonant-heavy. → Every alloy must hold ≥1 vowel + 1 consonant; late alloys pair a rare ore with iron/copper/stone (belted by then); the Constructor demands a measured pool of ≥25 real words before a recipe is offered.
  3. Union collapse. From T3 most unions ≈ the full set. → Strict union stops at Parts; deeper grammars use the full set + flux focus. Late variety comes from grammar and content pools, which is exactly the review the design wants.
  4. Material count. 6 ores + 8 smelter alloys + 5 foundry alloys + 6 generic = 25 ids. → Alloy icons are two/three-tone stacks of their ore colors; the in-canvas HUD goes contextual (what the dock needs), the full bag lives in the side panel.
  5. Recipes must be authored. If every ore pair were valid, outputs and names would explode. → Curated list; an unknown pair shows a ✗ row.
  6. Skill/economy drift. Materials could pile up faster than letters are ready, or the reverse. → Mk gates on readiness; tier builds on the previous tier bar; ⚙ on sticky letters; prices tuned so the economy finishes just after the bar. Skill is always the clock.
Honest caveat: the two T0 alloys are thin (9 syllables each) — that is the tutorial, and the first real words (тот, то, он, она, не) already appear inside them. And a strictly 1-in ore machine at Mk1 does not work here because iron is vowel-only at а о; the "one machine, different ore, different lesson" idea lives at the Constructor (one alloy in → one word pool out) and in every flux slot instead.

What this changes against v2, and what it needs

v2 → v3

  • 7 mines with fixed 4–5-letter sets → 6 ores with Mk1–Mk4, 2 keys per step, several nodes per ore. locked
  • Single-letter frequency unlocks → mirror-pair unlocks (same finger, both hands), frequency-sorted. locked
  • Fixed per-bench focus sets → alphabet computed from the recipe graph (union of inputs, live); ratio = focus weight.
  • Smelter 1-in → Smelter 2-in; Foundry (clusters) added; Crane (capitals) added; Circuit Fab dropped (rare words = Constructor on late alloys).
  • Punctuation as one machine → Fastener Mk1/2/3; period is mined (Oil Mk1); comma moves to T3. Capitals in at T5; numbers out.
  • Hub contracts + kits → everything built from your bag, at the place: hold Space opens the spot's menu, arrows choose (Mk at the mine, machines at plots, ⚙ at the machine, belts by spool). The Hub goes; a what-to-build-next checklist is later UI; contracts may return later for variety.
  • One global bag → machines have buffers; hand output rolls onto an exit belt if one exists; automated machines eat only from buffers; you can still collect and feed by hand.
  • Fixed BELTS list bought at an automated source → spool & socket from any machine to any accepting inlet, auto-routed, one belt per tile, choke points authored, distance-priced in the tier's trade good; pipes for oil.
  • Per-keystroke autofeed → automation runs in real time: belts, jobs, mines on the clock, bounded by buffers and by the one-tier ⚙ lag. Invariant 1 is amended: skill never advances without typing; mastered production may. Hand work still advances only its own station.
  • Automation only on tier-1 benches → one rule for every machine (⚙ ∧ sticky letters ∧ full buffers); processors get ⚙ one tier after arrival.
  • Depot, ₽ and Издание exams → removed for now. Tier bars are per-tier readiness targets on every unlocked letter (12 → 35 WPM-equivalent), so a tier opens the moment the letters are there — no station, no ceremony. Finishing the game ("launch" is just the word for it) = bar 35 + K heavy modules produced (the volume clock). Décor money and live exams can return later.
  • Fixed belt costs → belts free for now; automation and machines are the material sinks.
  • Save ids: additive only. az/buki/vedi/slogi/slova/stroki keep mapping to iron/copper/stone/…; display names live in i18n as today.
