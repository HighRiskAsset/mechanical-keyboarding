# Story concepts: the lore drip (brainstorm, 2026-09-16, for the user's rulings)

Status: concepts, not a plan. The ask (user, 2026-09-16): once the player is
typing whole sentences, what they type can carry a story. Someone is sending
messages through the machines. The player unravels it by paying attention,
follows a few small quests around the map, finds things disguised as scenery,
and learns that there was someone here before them. It must read like Outer
Wilds and Iconoclasts (assembled from observation, never explained, better
on the second run) with a turn in it like Motherload's (the voice that feeds
you turns). It must never cost the game anything as a typing tutor, it must
be authored per course rather than translated, and it waits for the lesson
order and durations to freeze. This is the biggest content addition after
the core.

Rejected before we start (user): the archaeology of the three pre-built
mines. It is unobservable and it only works in one language.

## 1. What the story has to obey

These are the game's own rules, and every concept below was checked against
them.

- **No dialogues after play starts.** No cards, no NPCs, no text boxes. The
  welcome card comes before play; the rest and finish cards are the only ones
  after. Icons carry information; the caption names what is focused.
- **The drill line is the only text channel during play.** Whatever the
  story says, the player says it first, with their fingers. This is not a
  limit. It is the whole opportunity: the game already asks the player to
  type what a machine shows them, and nobody has asked where the line comes
  from.
- **Progress is materials only.** Nothing in the story may lock a key, a
  machine or the finish. Story places sell rows for a price like any place;
  a story token is a material like any other. No skill gates, no flags that
  gate progress, no timers, no pressure.
- **Language is data.** Every typed beat is authored per course, graded to
  the rung it lands on, and constrained to the keys that rung has. The
  environment tells (a dark lamp, a lamp that breathes, a sign that faces
  the wrong way) are shared and language-free. The story is a bible of
  beats with constraints, not a text with translations.
- **Yield is not a mechanic; plenty is placement.** A story reward is a
  place to walk to and a line to type, never a rate.
- **Dread without urgency** (the music plan): danger is implied and never
  arrives. One unexplained motif that recurs and is never accounted for.
  The world reads slightly less bright than it does now.
- **The outskirts earn the walk.** The rim landmarks already exist for
  exactly this: the Summit, the flooded pit, the two crystal benches, the
  dry wash, the badland butte, the crevasse, the tar seep, the reed lagoon.
- **The finish is already a line of lore.** The finish card says: "Every
  machine hums without you." That sentence is the spine of every concept
  here. The goal of the game is to make the player unnecessary.

## 2. The channels: how a story can reach the player at all

| Channel | Language-free? | When | What it can carry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Word sets (rung: words, 15 EN lessons) | no | early-mid | recurring cryptic sets; the first "wait, again?" |
| Sentences and phrases (19 EN lessons) | no | mid | a voice in the first person; the I-to-you shift |
| Pages by category (20 EN: lore 3, let 3, dia 3, lit 3, triv 3, tech 5) | no | late | letters, transcripts, the machines talking, chosen excerpts |
| Rim landmarks and props (lamppost, sign, drum, crate) | yes | any | the quests' targets, disguised as scenery |
| Machine states (signal lamp dark, breathing, lit; idle poses) | yes | any | a machine that behaves as if worked by someone else |
| Sky: night, weather, the night run | yes | any | tells that show only at night |
| The music motif (three notes, unresolved) | yes | always | the signal; a lamp can breathe in its rhythm |
| Cards before and after play, the world picker taglines | no | edges | one planted sentence; a finish line that differs on the true route |
| The second world (Open Range) | no | replay | the same story from the other end |
| Live stats in a template slot (hours, keystrokes, letters) | shared | late | a letter that knows exactly how long you have been here |

The strongest channel is the first one that costs nothing: **the drill line
already is a message from the machine.** Every concept below uses it.

## 3. Five concepts

Each concept gives the premise, the question the player is chasing, the
voice, the turn, the endings, a sample drip, where it fits and where it
breaks.

### Concept 1: The one before (the relay)

**Premise.** The machines keep what is typed into them. Typing is the power
and the text is the fuel, and the ash stays in the machine. The lines the
machines show you are what they hold, and what they hold was typed by
whoever sat here before you. Early on it is worn to single words. As you
rebuild the frontier, the machines give back more of it, and it is a
person: they landed as you did, found the machines dormant, found older
words in them too, built everything up until every machine hummed without
them, and then had to decide what an operator does when nothing needs them.

**The question.** Who was here, what did they choose, and is it what I am
about to choose?

**The voice.** One person, unnamed (a name is a localization problem and a
community will name them anyway). First person, present tense, describing
the work you are doing as if they were doing it. Then the tense slips.

**The turn.** Companionable, then instructing, then asking. The late notes
send you to the rim (the small quests) and the last of them asks you for
something that is not obviously in your interest. Two readings stay open on
purpose: the warm one (they finished, nothing came, they typed a while for
the pleasure of it and walked out the way they marked for you) and the cold
one (they never left; the hum of the automated machines is their stored
typing, and it runs down; someone has to come back and sit).

**Endings.** Finish as normal: "every machine hums without you." True
route: the rim posts found in order, the last at the far edge, and the last
page at the Manufacturer is their unfinished letter, which you finish. The
finish card differs by one line.

**Sample drip.**
- words: `home hands rest` (innocent) · `same again still` · `who was here`
- sentences: `the smelter is warm again.` · `the wheel slows when the hands
  stop.` · `someone typed here before me. you will find my words too.`
- pages (lore): the note that names the first post: "the lamp that will not
  light."

**Fits.** Native to the mechanic; the pronoun shift from I to you exists in
every language; the pre-built mines get a use that is observable (one of
them keeps a lamp breathing at night, worked by nobody) and needs no words.

**Breaks.** Carries the whole game on one voice; if the writing is merely
fine, it is a diary. Needs the environment layer of concept 3 to breathe.

### Concept 2: Correspondence (the office that sent you)

**Premise.** You were sent. The letters category carries it: letters from
the office, cheerful, with quotas and thanks; then pressing; then wrong.
They address the previous operator by name. They ask for what was already
sent. They promise collection when the frontier is finished. The dialogue
category carries the previous operator's replies, written in the margins,
never answered.

**The question.** Who sent me, are they still there, and what is collected?

**The voice.** Two: the office (warm, then hollow) and the one before
(dry, tired, kind).

**The turn.** The office letters begin to know things only the game knows:
your hours, your keystrokes, which letters you have (a template slot the
finish card already uses). The office is not far away. The office is
written here, on the machine that makes pages. That is Motherload's turn
without a boss: the guide is the machine.

**Endings.** Finish: await collection (nothing arrives; the finish card as
it stands). True route: the return route the one before left on the rim,
and the last page is your own letter, the first letter you type that was
not written to you.

**Fits.** The letters and dialogue categories exist and are pages, so the
story lands exactly where the volume clock puts the player. Letters are a
form every language has.

**Breaks.** The company that turns is a worn trope (Motherload, FICSIT,
Aperture) and it slides into satire. The office must never be funny and
never be evil; it must simply not be there.

### Concept 3: The transmitter (what the machines are for)

**Premise.** Nobody has said what a heavy module is for. The frontier is
not a factory. It is a transmitter assembled one page at a time. The three
unresolved notes in every track are the signal it was built to send, and
the rim landmarks hold the receivers that have been listening for an
answer. Every post faces the same way.

**The question.** What is being sent, to whom, and should it be?

**The voice.** Almost none. The environment tells it: alignments, a lamp
that breathes in the motif's rhythm, the derrick, the Summit. The pages
carry only what a receiver caught.

**The turn.** The answer came a long time ago and was not for us; or the
answer is the word sets you typed at the start, played back.

**Endings.** Finish: the transmission completes. True route: you learn the
message and get to change it, which in this game means which last page you
type.

**Fits.** The most language-free concept; the environment does most of the
work, and the music plan's motif rule was written for it.

**Breaks.** Sending a message into the dark goes grand and preachy fast;
the payoff still has to be typed, and a fixed page is not a choice unless
the design makes it one (see 5.4).

### Concept 4: The machines are speaking (the lore category as the voice)

**Premise.** The machines are the voice. They were dormant because nobody
typed, and the first words they give you are the names of their own parts.
They learn. Each has a temperament that shows in its pages: the Smelter is
patient, the Manufacturer is the one that writes. They loved the one
before, who stayed.

**The question.** What do they want, and why did the last one stay?

**The turn.** "Every machine hums without you" and "keep the shift going"
are their wishes, not yours. The frontier keeps operators.

**Endings.** Finish: they can run without anybody, and they let you go.
True route: you find that they cannot, or that they could all along.

**Fits.** The A13 rules already list "lore (the machines talking)" as a
page category, so this is planned content given a spine.

**Breaks.** Talking machines tip to cute in one direction and to horror in
the other; the restraint required is the highest of the five. Best used as
one voice among others, not the spine.

### Concept 5: The print works (what the pages are)

**Premise.** The game was a print works before it was a frontier, and it
still is one under the iron: the Manufacturer prints. The one before was
writing something, one page per heavy module, and did not finish it. The K
pages you type at the end are its chapters. The literature, trivia and
technical pages are its quotations; the lore pages are its narrative.

**The question.** What is the book, and why did it stop?

**The turn.** The last chapters are about you.

**Endings.** Finish: the book is finished. True route: you add the page
that was missing.

**Fits.** Gives the finish a physical object and the mixed page categories
a reason to be mixed (a commonplace book).

**Breaks.** Loses the power of "every machine hums without you"; the finish
becomes a book instead of a machine. Localization is writing a different
book per language, the heaviest of the five.

## 4. Comparison and recommendation

| | 1 The one before | 2 Correspondence | 3 Transmitter | 4 Machines speak | 5 Print works |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uses the drill line as fiction | yes, natively | yes | weakly | yes | yes |
| Language-free layer | some | little | most | little | little |
| Motherload turn | yes | yes, strongest | no | yes | no |
| Outer Wilds environment | needs 3 | no | yes | no | no |
| Second-playthrough payoff | strong | medium | strong | weak | weak |
| Risk | one voice must be good | trope, satire | preachy | cute or horror | heavy to author |

**Recommendation: a fusion, working title "The Shift".** Concept 1 is the
spine (one person, before you, in the machines' memory). Concept 2 is the
second voice and the turn (the office letters that know your hours, and are
written here). Concept 3 is the language-free layer (the rim posts, the
motif, the lamp that breathes). Concept 4 stays as the lore category's own
pages, restrained: the machines talk, and they are not the plot. Concept 5
contributes one thing: the finish has an object, the pages.

The title works three ways and none of them are explained: the work shift,
the Shift key, and the shift from I to you. In the English course the
pronoun I cannot be typed until Shift is bought (the rules already hold the
pronoun back for the capital), so **the voice cannot say "I" until the
player can. The person appears with the capital.** The Russian course needs
its own beat for this moment (я is a plain letter); candidates are the dash
(dialogue arrives) or ё (the letter everyone leaves out, arriving late).
That is the model for every beat: same beat, per-course key.

### 4.1 The plot of The Shift, in full (never told in this order)

1. The frontier runs on typing because it is the one power a machine
   cannot make for itself. Someone has to sit down.
2. The machines keep what is typed into them and give it back as lines.
   The oldest layer is worn to single words (the word sets) and was typed
   by someone older than the one before. There have been at least three
   operators. Nobody says this; the register of the oldest words says it.
3. The one before arrived as you did, found three mines running (left for
   them, as they were left for you), found the old words, and built the
   frontier up. The three-note motif is something they hummed at the
   machines; the machines kept that too.
4. The office wrote to them: quotas, thanks, the promise of collection at
   the finish. They wrote back. The office never answered a reply. The
   letters got stranger and then began quoting the operator's own numbers.
   The office is the Manufacturer. The letters were always made here.
5. They finished. Every machine hummed without them. Nothing came.
6. What they did next is the game's one real ambiguity, and the two maps
   hold contradicting evidence on purpose. The warm reading: they typed a
   while for the pleasure of it, marked a way out along the rim for
   whoever came next, and walked it. The cold reading: automation runs on
   stored typing and it runs down; they stayed to keep the hum, and the
   lamp that breathes on the starting mine at night is them, still at it.
7. You are the next one. On the true route, the last page you type is
   their unfinished letter to you, and you finish it. What you typed at the
   rim posts is what the machines will give the next operator. (In code
   nothing crosses worlds; in the reader's head everything does, see 5.5.)

### 4.2 What is never explained (the wiki list)

One rule from the music plan applies to the whole story: **one thing is
never accounted for.** The rest is answerable by a careful second run.

- Who typed the oldest words (answerable: an older operator, by register).
- Why the three starting mines run (answerable: left running by the one
  before, and one keeps a lamp breathing at night).
- What the office is (answerable: the Manufacturer, by the numbers it knows).
- Whether the one before left or stayed (answerable both ways, by design,
  from the two maps).
- What a heavy module is for (answerable on the Range only).
- Why the machines are steampunk and where the frontier is (never).

## 5. The drip: how The Shift reaches the player

Everything below is a sketch to argue with, not a plan; it waits for the
lesson order and durations to freeze.

### 5.1 The timeline by rung (EN columns as they stand today)

| Rung (cols) | What the player types | What the story does |
|---|---|---|
| streams, syllables (1 to 8) | letters and syllables | nothing. Not one wink. A beat here would cost the lesson. |
| words (about 5 to 12) | word lists | three or four sets recur across lessons: `home hands rest` (innocent) · `same again still` · `who was here` · `not alone`. Rule: every story set must also be the best pedagogical set for its keys, or it does not go in. |
| sentences (about 9 to 20) | short sentences | the voice, impersonal at first (`the wheel slows when the hands stop.`), then "I" the moment Shift arrives, then the tense slips (`the smelter was warm when i came.`), then the address (`you will find my words too.`). |
| phrases, full (20 to 22) | longer, punctuated | the first quest is named obliquely: "the lamp that will not light." |
| pages (22 to 31) | letters, dialogue, lore, lit, triv, tech | the office letters (three, escalating, the third quotes your hours); the dialogue pages are the replies in the margin; the lore pages are the machines; the real excerpts are chosen to rhyme (telegraphy, lighthouses, letters never answered, the QWERTY story); the last page differs on the true route. |

The environment starts on the first night, long before the words do: a
lamppost by the landing that stays dark when every other lamp lights; a
starting mine whose signal lamp breathes with nobody at it; a sign that
faces the rim instead of the road. The player who notices these on day one
has been hooked without a word.

### 5.2 The posts

A post is a piece of scenery with a secret: an existing prop kind
(lamppost, sign, drum, crate) or a landmark, standing where the outskirts
already put the interesting ground. What gives it away is language-free
and shows at night or under weather: the lamp that will not light, the
lamp that breathes in the motif's rhythm, the drum in the pond, the sign
facing the wrong way, the flooded pit that reflects a lamp that is not
there.

Mechanically a post is a place, and it obeys every rule places have: walk
up, the caption names it (the brass line names the row, the second line
describes it, and it never names an absence), hold Space, one row, priced.
Buying the row is typing one line graded to the player's current rung, so
the post is a lesson. Its output is a material (a tape, a letter, a card;
the naming pass decides). The next post's row is priced in the previous
post's material. That is the quest chain with no new mechanic: places,
rows, prices, materials. After the line, the post's lamp lights and stays
lit, so the rim brightens as the story is found, and a returning player can
see at a glance what they have and have not read.

Six lands around the meadow, a post in each, and the last at the far edge
(the Summit, the ground the outskirts ruling built for a reason). The posts
are never required. The finish does not need one.

### 5.3 Hooking early

- The first night: the dark lamp, the breathing mine, the wrong-way sign.
- Word sets that recur; by the third, a player who reads what they type
  has a question.
- The welcome card gains nothing. It already says every machine runs on
  typing, which turns out to be the whole story; leave it exactly as it is
  and let the player reread it at the end.

### 5.4 The choice at the end

A fixed page is not a choice. The true route makes it one without a
dialogue: the last post's row is priced in every earlier post's material,
and what it sells is the last page. Buy it and the Manufacturer's final
page is the one before's letter instead of the office's. Do not, and it is
the office's. Both finish the game. The finish card changes one line and
nothing else. No branch, no flag, no gate: a price, paid or not.

### 5.5 The second world

Open Range is "one wide plain, every vein in a row, and room to build
wherever you like." It is the tidy world, and it tells the story from the
other end. Two options, not decided:

- **The Range is where the one before came from.** Its word sets are their
  earliest words; its letters are the office's drafts, written before they
  were sent. A player who finished the Frontier's true route reads the
  Range's first sentences and knows who is speaking. A player who did not
  sees cryptic words.
- **The Range is where they went.** The way out marked on the Frontier's
  rim leads here. Its first sentence is the last line of the Frontier's
  true-route letter.

Either way, nothing crosses in code (one save per world, nothing shared),
and everything crosses in the reader's head, which is the Iconoclasts
effect: the second run makes the first make sense.

### 5.6 Localization is authoring, not translation

The story ships as a bible of beats. Each beat has: its purpose, the rung
it lands on, the keys it may use, its language-free tell if it has one,
and a reference rendering. Every course writes its own text to the beat.
The English and Russian courses will not say the same sentences, and the
person appearing with Shift in English is a different moment in Russian.
The environment, the posts, the lamp, the motif and the live-stat slots are
shared and cost nothing per language. Russian keeps its dashes.

### 5.7 Build notes for later, not now

- The bot plans from CHAIN and will price the posts like any row; either
  it buys them (fine, it is a lesson) or the rows are marked optional.
- The night run already darkens the keyboard; a post that shows only at
  night pairs with it naturally.
- The map thumbnails are baked; a lit post on the picker thumbnail would
  need a re-bake per state, so the thumbnail shows the map, never the
  story.

## 6. What to decide next

1. Which spine: the fusion as sketched, or one of the five alone.
2. The one thing never explained (proposed: where the frontier is and why
   the iron is steampunk).
3. Warm and cold readings both kept, or one chosen.
4. Which second-world option (5.5).
5. Then, after the lesson order and durations freeze: the beat bible, one
   row per beat, with its rung and keys, before any sentence is written.
